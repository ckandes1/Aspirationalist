# AR research-publication plan decision

Status: Project source, not canonical guidance  
Captured: 2026-07-30  
Source: Planning conversation refining the AR Advisory GTM project

## Source purpose

Preserve the reasoning that converted the project from a generic sequence of buyer interviews and free advisory engagements into a specific research-publication mechanism with optional advisory follow-up.

## Problem with the earlier outreach concept

The initial outreach language said Keith was researching a future advisory offer and would be happy to help with a live MQ issue.

Keith rejected that framing because it resembled the presales and service-discovery messages he receives on LinkedIn. Even when the sender says nothing is being sold, the message can still feel like a disguised funnel. That creates avoidable skepticism and reduces response rates among the people most sensitive to sales outreach.

The project needed a reason for the conversation that stood on its own and created real value regardless of any future consulting offer.

## Publication as the entry mechanism

Keith proposed interviewing AR practitioners for an article or publication about how AR teams currently handle the Magic Quadrant.

The publication gives the participant a clear role:

- contribute practitioner experience;
- help develop a useful industry perspective;
- receive the findings; and
- potentially gain visibility or attribution if desired.

It also fits Keith's existing strength and interest in publishing content.

The working article angle became:

**What AR Leaders Wish Executives Understood About the Magic Quadrant**

The article should explore where executive expectations create friction, wasted effort, or poor decisions and what experienced AR practitioners believe companies should approach differently.

## Correct order of the participant experience

The advisory session should not appear in the initial invitation.

The agreed sequence is:

1. Invite the person only to contribute to the article.
2. Conduct the publication interview.
3. Ask whether there is anyone else in AR who should be interviewed on the topic.
4. Then offer a separate free advisory session as a thank-you for their time.
5. If accepted, gather any additional private context needed to prepare.
6. Run the session around a real MQ or executive-stakeholder issue.
7. Follow up to learn whether the advice was used and what happened.
8. Offer one additional short call if useful.

This order protects the legitimacy of the interview and allows the advisory session to be received as a genuine return of value.

## Inquiry-style advisory model

Keith compared the free session to a Gartner inquiry.

The session should:

- begin with the participant's real situation and question;
- use discussion and direct advice rather than a prebuilt consulting package;
- help the participant interpret the situation, test assumptions, and choose an action; and
- preserve flexibility while Keith learns where his perspective is most useful.

The important difference is Keith's ability to follow up directly. He can ask whether the participant used the advice, what changed, and whether another short conversation would help. That downstream evidence is central to service validation.

## Validation distinctions

The project should track four separate layers of evidence:

### Content validation

People accept interviews, provide useful insights, refer peers, approve quotes, share the article, or attract publisher interest.

### Problem validation

Similar triggers, executive misunderstandings, consequences, and unmet needs appear independently across interviews.

### Service validation

A participant brings a consequential problem, uses Keith's advice, and changes a decision or action.

### Offer validation

A participant requests continued help, refers a peer based on the advisory experience, introduces a buyer, discusses willingness to pay, or joins a paid beta.

Keith specifically wanted these distinctions operationalized in a tracker or retrospective so the project does not confuse politeness, content engagement, or interview participation with proof of a service.

## Two publication versions

One article cannot cleanly serve both AR practitioners and B2B SaaS executives.

The research should produce two separate outputs:

1. An AR practitioner article focused on executive expectations, program pressure, resource allocation, and practitioner advice.
2. An executive article translating those findings into product investment, differentiation, executive alignment, credibility, and commercial impact.

## External distribution before self-publication

Keith's prior experience with contributed articles suggests many external publications want original or exclusive work. Publishing the full article on LinkedIn first could remove those opportunities.

The agreed distribution sequence is:

1. Finish the article draft.
2. Identify relevant AR, PMM, marketing, PR, B2B SaaS, and executive outlets or organizations.
3. Pitch the strongest target before public release.
4. Offer the article and adjacent formats such as a podcast, webinar, panel, roundtable, or research discussion.
5. Give the target a clear response window.
6. Move to the next outlet or self-publish if the opportunity does not proceed.

Identifying the outlet and organization list is now an explicit future project task.

## Important cautions

- A publication can become a productive-looking avoidance mechanism if it never leads to live advisory situations.
- Mentioning the advisory session in the initial outreach would make the publication feel like funnel camouflage.
- Article attribution and private advisory confidentiality need separate permissions.
- AR agencies may be valuable contributors or distribution partners, but their adjacent commercial interests make research independence important.
- Positive feedback does not establish service validation.

## Current conclusion

The project now has a concrete mechanism that can begin with one conversation:

`article interview → referral → optional advisory session → impact follow-up`

The mechanism can expand into a research publication, two audience-specific articles, external distribution opportunities, and eventually a paid beta if the advisory evidence supports one.
