# 2026 MQ Season Notes

Status: Source document, not canonical guidance  
Last consolidated: 2026-07-07  
Coverage: Vendor-side Gartner Magic Quadrant cycle through fact review and publication preparation

## Why this document exists

I ran the 2026 MQ cycle with useful experience on the analyst side but without a reusable vendor-side operating package. The work happened, the submission got done, and the lessons accumulated across planning notes, dictation, deck slides, stickies, retrospective drafts, and working documents.

The failure was not simply that the cycle was messy. The larger failure would be letting the mess disappear without converting it into a better system.

This document is the consolidated source record. It is intentionally more candid, repetitive, and contextual than the eventual MQ Package. The top section is usable now. The lower section preserves the reasoning and rough notes that explain where the conclusions came from.

This file does not reproduce licensed Gartner content, evaluation details, customer information, private company data, or confidential facts. It preserves the operational learning while generalizing details that do not belong in a public repository.

# Part I: Usable synthesis

## The 2026 cycle in one paragraph

The cycle started cold. I believed there was little meaningful work to do before the Welcome Packet, inherited almost no reusable process from the prior attempt, and entered a compressed evaluation without a mapped team, staged tools, or a packet-day operating plan. Once the work arrived, I had to build the process while running it. Stakeholders needed more direction than I gave them, the coordinator became the structural bottleneck, and the project repeatedly moved into the next stage before I documented the last one. We still completed the questionnaire, demo, submission, and fact review. The largest output now needs to be the package that prevents the next cold start.

## Conditions that shaped the cycle

Three conditions explain much of what happened:

1. **No inherited package.** The prior attempt ended partway through the process, and the useful materials, decisions, and lessons were not organized for reuse.
2. **A compressed timeline.** The 2026 cycle was materially shorter than the comparable prior cycle. There was less room to learn, orient people, or recover from delay.
3. **One coordinator, not an AR team.** Coordination, analyst judgment, synthesis, prior-year comparison, editing, and narrative coherence concentrated in one person.

Those conditions do not excuse the misses. They explain why the operating system must be built around the coordinator bottleneck, early delegation, and stage-level memory.

## What the MQ work actually consists of

The working model that emerged is broader than “complete the questionnaire.”

- **Year-round performance:** company execution, strategy, product delivery, customer proof, commercial results, evidence capture, and analyst credibility.
- **Pre-season activation:** re-enter the context, prepare the team, stage tools, explain the project, and remove packet-day decisions.
- **Evaluation season:** coordinate and package the year of performance accurately, coherently, and credibly.
- **Fact review:** correct facts and protect important nuance without pretending the evaluation can be rebuilt.
- **Publication and value capture:** turn the result into credibility, enablement, campaigns, and next-cycle decisions.
- **Post-season:** convert the experience and analyst feedback into the next operating plan while the detail is still fresh.

The submission period is the most visible phase. It is not where the underlying result is created.

## Stage 0: Before the Welcome Packet

### What happened

I treated the pre-packet period like a waiting room. I knew the MQ was coming, but I did not have a clear map of useful preparation work. I was rusty, did not review the prior questionnaire deeply enough, did not have a reusable package, and did not begin team orientation early.

There was a prior attempt, but it had ended in disqualification before the full cycle. Because that experience was not closed with a proper retrospective and package update, I effectively started again from scratch.

### What it cost

- The first post-packet week contained orientation and tool-building that should have happened earlier.
- I had to reconstruct the project plan while the clock was already running.
- Stakeholder assignments and expectations were reactive.
- The coordinator was learning the shape of the project at the same time everyone else needed direction.
- Work that would have felt premature several weeks earlier became obviously necessary once there was no time left for it.

### What I learned

There is substantial work before the packet even though the exact questionnaire is unknown.

The pre-season job is not to guess every new question. It is to warm up the system:

- review the prior report, questionnaire, demo, and retrospective
- remember the question areas and likely owners
- write a short market and vendor prediction to reactivate analytical thinking
- refresh stakeholder ownership and availability
- define the executive sponsor and escalation path
- stage schedules, trackers, workspaces, and communications
- train stakeholders on what is coming and why the process will feel imperfect
- remind executives what the MQ can and cannot be influenced at each stage

The idea of reserving roughly 5–10 hours per week for several weeks initially felt excessive because the new packet was not available. In hindsight, that time would have changed the cycle.

### Requirement for next cycle

Pre-season becomes a real stage with entry criteria, outputs, owners, and a written retro.

### Unresolved

- Is the right activation window four weeks, six weeks, or eight weeks?
- Which preparation belongs before criteria confirmation versus after it?
- Should stakeholder education happen entirely pre-packet, or should a short refresher also happen after packet receipt?

## Stage 1: Welcome Packet and first week

### What happened

The packet arrived and I did not immediately respond as though a starting gun had fired. There was no packet-day script. I had to turn estimated dates into a real setback schedule, centralize materials, compare documents, build context, identify owners, and communicate urgency in real time.

### What it cost

- The first week was partly lost.
- Context transfer began too late.
- Stakeholders encountered a large questionnaire before the work had been made legible.
- The project felt more chaotic because several setup decisions were made after work had already started.

### What I learned

Packet Day should not require fresh project-design decisions. It needs a prewritten response.

Immediate work should include:

- clear the coordinator’s calendar
- send the tiger-team communication
- turn the estimated timeline into the planned setback schedule
- place all received documents in the central workspace
- schedule the kickoff
- confirm questionnaire and demo ownership
- compare the portal with the workbook
- begin the prior-year line-by-line comparison
- start stakeholder tagging
- begin daily inbox and spam checks for analyst communications

### Requirement for next cycle

Create a one-page Packet Day checklist and communication set before the packet arrives.

### Unresolved

- Which actions must happen on Day 0, Day 1, and by the end of Week 1?
- How much of the prior-year comparison must be complete before kickoff?

## Stage 2: Questionnaire

### What happened

I distributed the questionnaire with too little guidance. The practical message to some contributors was close to “go into the document and figure out your area.” That annoyed people and created avoidable questions.

I was cautious about tagging individual people because I worried they would only review tagged questions. The caution produced under-tagging. In practice, contributors wanted clearer direction even when the assignment could not be perfect.

The workbook and portal were not identical. Some apparent optional fields in the workbook did not exist in the portal, so effort went into answers that had nowhere to go.

Contributions arrived across functions while I was also trying to understand the whole document, help people, chase gaps, compare against prior years, and preserve one company story.

### What it cost

- Contributors felt overwhelmed and under-directed.
- Time was wasted writing material for unavailable fields.
- My coordination time competed with my processing and synthesis time.
- Some contributors prioritized normal work over the evaluation until executives reset the priority.
- The final centralization burden grew because pieces were not always returned and processed continuously.

### What I learned

#### Audit before distribution

The portal is the actual submission environment. The workbook is a working aid. They must be compared before the team writes into the workbook.

#### Give imperfect direction

Tag people question by question where possible and by section where necessary. State clearly that tags are a starting point and each functional owner must scan beyond them.

#### Explain the operating model

This is not a normal collaboration project optimized for minimal stakeholder burden. In a short cycle with one coordinator and real inclusion or placement risk, the correct model is early mass delegation followed by coordinator tightening.

Trying to make the process polished and easy for every stakeholder transfers more work to the coordinator, who is already the bottleneck.

#### Keep executives accountable

Executives can delegate drafting, but they retain responsibility for the completeness and quality of their functional sections. The coordinator should not be afraid to delegate work upward.

#### Receive work continuously

Pieces should return as they are ready. The coordinator needs to process material while other work continues, identify gaps early, and avoid one final batch.

### Requirement for next cycle

- portal/workbook audit before distribution
- question-level or section-level owner mapping
- all-stakeholder kickoff
- offered one-on-one support
- pre-agreed escalation path
- continuous return of contributions
- recurring gap sweep
- protected coordinator processing blocks

### Unresolved

- How granular should tagging become before the return diminishes?
- What is the best balance between office hours, one-on-ones, and async support?
- How often should gap sweeps occur in a very short cycle?
- Which contributor work can be assisted safely by AI, and what review controls are required?

## Stage 3: Demo

### What happened

Demo work ran in parallel with the questionnaire. Multiple people contributed pieces, concerns appeared during the build, and the prior demo was provided as a reference after work had begun.

The most useful operational asset was an existing demo tracker built by the product leader, but it had not yet been converted into a reusable package asset.

### What it cost

The demo process is the least documented part of the 2026 cycle. That lack of memory is itself a process failure. It is difficult to distinguish what worked, what was improvised, and what should become standard.

### What I learned

- Confirm demo ownership before the packet.
- Make prior demos and scoring context available immediately.
- Templatize the strongest existing tracker.
- Start demo work as aggressively as questionnaire work.
- Apply continuous flow, gap review, and escalation to demo components.
- Preserve demo-specific decisions and lessons at the end of the stage.

### Requirement for next cycle

A dedicated demo playbook cannot be completed from the current source set. It needs a focused retrospective with the people who built and reviewed the demo.

### Unresolved

- What were the actual demo workstreams and handoffs?
- Which problems appeared midstream?
- How did the tracker work, and what was missing from it?
- What review passes are required for storyline, proof, timing, and production quality?
- What should be recorded immediately after the demo is complete?

## Stage 4: Centralize, review, and submit

### What happened

The questionnaire and demo were completed and submitted on time. The hardest part concentrated near the end: checking coherence, comparing against previous years, locating thin or unanswered areas, resolving contradictions, editing, and making the submission tell one credible story.

### What it cost

This stage exposed the unavoidable part of the coordinator bottleneck. The coordinator cannot delegate the full-story view without fragmenting the submission. When too much upstream work remains unresolved, the centralization stage expands until it consumes all available time.

### What I learned

There is one category of work that should remain centralized:

- whole-submission coherence
- prior-year comparison
- alignment to the actual corporate strategy
- credibility and claim calibration
- final gap killing

Everything else should be pushed outward as early as possible.

The coordinator needs protected time for this stage. Meetings, administrative requests, avoidable contributor questions, and unrelated projects should be reduced aggressively.

### Requirement for next cycle

Define centralization as a formal gate with protected coordinator-only blocks, clear inputs, a final review sequence, and exit criteria.

### Unresolved

- How many protected days or half-days are realistic?
- What must functional executives sign off before centralization begins?
- What automated checks or AI workflows can surface contradictions without weakening human judgment?

## Stage 5: Immediate post-submission period

### What happened

The project moved straight from submission into the next set of obligations. A short stage retrospective did not happen while memories were fresh.

By July, I was reconstructing the cycle from scattered notes, stickies, decks, and dictation. I could remember the overall frustration and important lessons, but some stage details, especially demo work, were already thin.

### What it cost

- Lessons became harder to retrieve and verify.
- Emotional memory remained stronger than operational detail.
- The next package had to be created by aggregation instead of simple maintenance.
- Open loops from one stage bled into the next.

### What I learned

Every gate needs a brief written retro before the team moves on.

A useful 15-minute version may be:

- What was supposed to happen?
- What actually happened?
- What helped?
- What broke or surprised us?
- What changes before the next gate?
- What must be added to the package before memory fades?

### Requirement for next cycle

No stage closes without a short written retro. The full post-season retrospective should aggregate stage notes, not recreate the cycle from scratch.

### Unresolved

- Should the retro be coordinator-only first, then team-reviewed?
- Which stages need the executive team present, and which should deliberately exclude them?

## Stage 6: Fact review

### What happened

When the draft profile and scores arrived, executives moved into “fix it” mode. Placement, cautions, and wording produced negative reactions and pressure to make changes at a point where the evaluation was almost complete.

I had not sufficiently preset expectations before the draft arrived.

### What it cost

- The meeting risked becoming a grievance session.
- Energy went toward arguments that could not change the report materially.
- Internal knowledge and internal bias were treated as though they overruled the analyst’s external judgment.
- Negative reaction threatened to carry into publication and reduce the company’s willingness to use the result.

### What I learned

Fact review needs psychological preparation as much as document preparation.

Roughly two weeks before the expected draft, executives should hear:

- the evaluation is approximately complete
- every vendor dislikes parts of its evaluation
- the report is the analyst firm’s opinion
- fact review is for factual errors and a small number of materially misleading nuances
- strategic disagreement becomes next-cycle work
- the company will need to accept, use, and celebrate the final result

On receipt, the coordinator should first classify the draft:

- positives
- negatives
- factual errors
- judgment calls
- things the company can affect now
- things that belong in the next cycle

### Requirement for next cycle

Create a pre-fact-review executive script, a review scorecard, decision rules for proposed changes, and a structured submission process.

### Unresolved

- What threshold makes a nuance worth fighting for?
- Who has final authority when executives want to challenge a judgment call?
- How should disagreement be captured for Zone 1 without allowing it to derail fact review?

## Stage 7: Publication preparation and value capture

### What happened as of July 7

Fact review was complete and publication was pending. Campaign preparation and staff enablement were underway. The exact publication date remained uncertain.

The immediate risk was organizational carryover from fact-review disappointment. The company had spent the effort, but value would only begin when the report was published and actively used.

### What I learned

Publication needs a deliberate pivot from correction to leverage.

Before publication, the team should have trigger-ready materials rather than a plan dependent on one exact date:

- internal and executive announcements
- team congratulations
- website news post
- explanatory article or asset
- registrant and prospect email
- sales enablement
- competitive framing
- regional campaign waves
- companion Critical Capabilities explanation

On publication day, the message must become:

> The evaluation is complete. Our job now is to use it to build trust, support deals, and create demand.

The coordinator also has to model celebration. If the person closest to the evaluation acts disappointed, the rest of the organization will treat the result as unusable.

### Requirement for the current cycle

- continue daily analyst-email and spam checks until publication
- finalize the publication-day sequence and owners
- prepare the executive pivot message
- coordinate timing with adjacent analyst publications
- explicitly congratulate the team
- run the campaign and enablement sequence
- redirect late correction requests into the next-cycle plan

### Unresolved

- How should value be measured across pipeline, conversion, sales use, and brand credibility?
- Should closely timed analyst publications be combined into one story or staggered?
- How long should campaign activity continue after publication?

## Stage 8: Post-season and return to year-round work

### What needs to happen

This stage was not complete when this source document was created.

Post-season should include two distinct retrospectives:

1. **Project retrospective:** how the evaluation was run
2. **Business and product retrospective:** what the evaluation says about company performance, strategy, evidence, roadmap, market position, and intentional disagreements with the analyst view

Required work includes:

- analyst feedback call
- Critical Capabilities and profile analysis
- project retrospective
- product and company implications
- roadmap, positioning, investment, and evidence recommendations
- executive and stakeholder wrap-up
- package and template updates
- next-cycle ownership and cadence
- campaign continuation and measurement

### Open-next-year-plan timing

The sources contain a useful tension:

- One early workflow note says to open next year’s plan immediately after submission.
- Later synthesis places the formal action after publication and feedback, when the cycle is actually complete.

The likely resolution is:

- capture provisional next-cycle actions immediately after submission
- formally open and approve the next-cycle plan during post-season, after publication and analyst feedback

This preserves momentum without treating an incomplete cycle as final.

# Cross-stage conclusions

## 1. The coordinator is the structural bottleneck

The coordinator’s scarcest resource is not meeting time. It is judgment and processing time.

The coordinator must understand the entire evaluation, maintain analyst credibility, integrate cross-functional contributions, compare against prior cycles, and make the submission coherent. Those duties cannot be distributed casually.

Therefore, the operating system must:

- delegate content creation early
- keep executives accountable for functional outputs
- return pieces continuously
- escalate delays quickly
- protect synthesis time
- reduce avoidable formatting and administrative work
- stage tools before the packet arrives

## 2. Optimize for the actual situation

A mature market leader with a full AR team, predictable inclusion, and years of reusable material may optimize for efficiency and low stakeholder burden.

A challenger with one AR lead, a compressed cycle, and less reusable infrastructure needs a different model: get everyone engaged quickly, accept some early mess, and tighten centrally.

The wrong optimization target creates a beautiful process that fails at the only constraint that matters.

## 3. Executive alignment is continuous

The same misunderstanding appeared at both ends of the cycle:

- early: insufficient urgency and ownership
- late: excessive belief that the result could still be changed

Executive alignment belongs throughout the lifecycle:

- year-round company performance and evidence
- pre-season education and priority
- packet-day urgency
- mid-season escalation
- pre-fact-review expectation setting
- publication pivot
- post-season business decisions

## 4. Organizational psychology is operational work

Predictable emotional reactions caused operational problems:

- coordinator rust and paralysis before the packet
- contributor overwhelm when facing the questionnaire
- resistance to imperfect delegation
- executive anger at the draft
- disappointment threatening publication use

The package needs scripts, training, and decision rules that handle these reactions before they become emergencies.

## 5. Every action needs its reason

A checklist without reasons will be abandoned under pressure, especially a year after it was written.

Important actions should explain:

- what failure they prevent
- why the timing matters
- who is protected by the action
- what happens if it is skipped

## 6. Retrospectives are part of execution

The retrospective is not an optional end-of-season essay. It is the mechanism that allows the program to compound.

Each gate needs a short capture. Post-season aggregates and formalizes those captures. The package is updated while the people and details are still available.

## 7. The corporate story cannot be invented during submission

The MQ narrative must be the company strategy already being executed and the story already being discussed with analysts.

A disconnected submission story creates contradictions across questionnaire answers, demo claims, roadmap, customer evidence, executive statements, and analyst memory.

## 8. The package is one of the primary outputs of every cycle

The evaluation result matters. The operating capability left behind also matters.

The package should eventually retain:

- lifecycle definitions
- stage checklists with reasons
- stakeholder maps and RACI
- setback schedules
- Packet Day plan
- portal/workbook audit
- questionnaire comparison method
- demo tracker
- communications and scripts
- training
- fact-review scorecard
- publication sequence
- AI workspace guidance
- stage retrospectives
- source inventory and version history

# Part II: Candid source library

This section preserves the rougher reasoning behind the synthesis. It includes repetition because repeated concerns often reveal the real design constraint.

## A. Why aggregation became urgent

The immediate feeling in July was not simply “I need a retrospective.” It was that the entire work system had become mentally undifferentiated.

The notes were everywhere. The project kept crossing gates without a pause. Mistakes were visible, but they were not written down clearly enough to become improvements. That created the fear that the cycle would end and the lessons would disappear again.

The raw concern was:

> Everything feels messy. I have notes everywhere and loose ends I have not looped back. There are different stages of MQ season, and after each stage I probably should have done a retrospective for that gate. I did not because the project moved immediately into the next thing.

The emotional layer matters because it explains the design requirement. A future package cannot rely on the coordinator feeling calm, organized, and able to reconstruct the full cycle afterward. The system has to externalize memory during the work.

Another raw conclusion was more direct:

> I must have an MQ program package coming out of MQ season. This is the most important thing I could do in the next three months.

The package is not cleanup. It is how one difficult cycle becomes accumulated operating capability instead of another isolated event.

## B. First-cycle vendor-side reality

Prior analyst experience created real advantages, but it did not remove the learning curve of running the evaluation from inside a vendor.

The work required different muscles:

- building internal urgency
- assigning cross-functional work
- getting executives to own sections
- handling incomplete evidence
- coordinating a demo
- protecting synthesis time
- translating analyst judgment for emotionally invested leaders
- converting publication into business value

The candid fear was that the cycle felt like evidence of failure. The more useful interpretation is that the first full vendor-side cycle exposed the operating system that had been missing.

The crucial rule that emerged from the dictation process was:

> Capture first. Structure, action, and simplification come later.

That principle should remain in the package-development process itself. Source notes can be messy. The canonical operating system cannot be built if capture is blocked by the demand for polish.

## C. Raw April planning observations

The April notes were written while the questionnaire and demo were active. They contain the clearest in-the-moment record of the bottleneck.

### Portal and workbook mismatch

- Not every question actually had the optional additional-information field.
- The portal needed to be checked and the workbook marked before people wrote responses that could not be submitted.
- This was a small technical mismatch with disproportionate wasted effort.

### Tagging and contributor guidance

- People were understandably overwhelmed by the questionnaire.
- They benefited from direction about where to look, even when the assignment was imperfect.
- Person-by-person tagging was preferable where possible.
- Tags could not be treated as exhaustive, so contributors still needed to scan beyond them.
- A more hands-on start was needed: prep, an all-stakeholder kickoff, and one-on-one help.
- The exact level of tagging granularity was unresolved because there are diminishing returns.

### Pre-season work that became obvious too late

The notes identified work that could have happened before the packet:

- kickoff and RACI
- prior-year retrospective and lessons review
- training on questionnaire, demo, responsibilities, project plan, and tentative timing
- organizational priority message
- separate explanation of MQ strategy and influence levers
- staged schedules and materials

The main contradiction was psychological: this work felt premature before the packet, then became obviously necessary once the packet arrived.

### Coordinator processing versus coordination

The in-cycle anxiety centered on a real constraint:

- Time spent helping and coordinating contributors reduced time available to process and synthesize their answers.
- Time spent processing reduced availability for the people who needed guidance.
- The project architecture was being redesigned while the project itself continued moving.
- Pieces needed to come back continuously so processing could occur across the whole window.

### Winning versus efficiency

The strongest April conclusion was that the project should optimize for winning, not generic project efficiency.

A process designed to reduce effort for every stakeholder can increase the burden on the coordinator. When the coordinator is the scarcest resource, that is the wrong trade.

The rough formulation was:

> We needed to get all hands on deck quickly so we could brute-force this, not make everything polished and efficient for every stakeholder. I need people in immediately, and then I tighten it up.

That language is intentionally blunt. The canonical package may refine it, but it should not lose the underlying tradeoff.

### Trust and stakeholder framing

Every stakeholder sees only part of the evaluation. The coordinator needs contributors to trust that some imperfect-seeming choices are deliberate optimizations based on how analysts evaluate the submission.

The kickoff should say:

- this is large and time-compressed
- one person is integrating the whole submission
- contributors will receive direction, but not always a perfectly packaged assignment
- executives remain accountable when they delegate
- one-on-one help is available
- the team must move before every ambiguity is resolved

### Delegating to executives

The notes repeatedly returned to discomfort about assigning work upward.

The conclusion was that discomfort is irrelevant. Executives own the company claims and functional evidence. The coordinator adds context, coherence, and analyst judgment, but cannot personally create all of the evidence or answers.

### Prior-year comparison

A line-by-line comparison near the beginning could take two hours or most of a day. The notes still judged it worthwhile because it:

- makes the coordinator fluent
- identifies reusable answers
- exposes new and conflicting questions
- supports tagging
- gives executives a concrete scope signal

The 2026 analysis suggested a high share of new questions. Whether the exact percentage is used publicly is less important than preserving the comparison method.

## D. Retrospective reconstruction

The structured retrospective later organized the cycle into seven observed stages:

1. pre-season
2. packet arrival
3. questionnaire
4. demo
5. centralize and submit
6. fact review
7. publication and campaign

It identified five root causes:

1. no inherited package
2. coordinator bottleneck
3. stakeholder and decision-maker alignment
4. late urgency
5. actions without reasons

That retrospective is useful, but it is already one level of synthesis. It should not erase the raw notes that show how those causes were discovered.

## E. Workflow draft and later corrections

The gated workflow translated lessons into actions with a reason attached to each action. Its strongest design decisions were:

- Zone 1 feeds Zone 2.
- Pre-season is Gate 0.
- Packet Day is scripted.
- Questionnaire and demo work return continuously.
- Centralization is protected coordinator work.
- Fact review gets expectation-setting before the draft.
- Publication materials are trigger-ready.
- Every gate ends with a short written retro.

The workflow was a valuable skeleton, but it should not yet be treated as canonical. Several items still need testing, especially demo work, exact timing, retro format, and the formal start of next-cycle planning.

## F. Publication-period punch list

As of the consolidation date, the live operational list included:

- daily analyst inbox and spam review
- post-publication executive pivot message
- trigger-ready publication sequence
- coordination with other expected analyst publications
- campaign wave planning
- stakeholder wrap-up scheduling
- holding fact-review scope
- redirecting late correction energy into the next cycle
- explicit team celebration after publication

This remains time-sensitive current-cycle work rather than a final package design.

## G. Zone 1 / Zone 2 source thinking

The earlier Zone notes framed the work this way:

- Zone 2 is the visible evaluation season: preparation, packet, questionnaire, demo, review, publication, and immediate post-season.
- Zone 1 is the always-on period: company execution plus focused analyst intelligence and relationships.
- AR has an inbound role, not only an outbound story role.
- Analyst signals should affect business thesis, roadmap, and execution.
- By packet receipt, much of the underlying result has already been created.

The original loop was:

`analyst intelligence → business thesis → roadmap → execution → product and evidence → analyst evaluation`

This is a useful source model. The exact Zone boundaries still need refinement because post-season and pre-season overlap the transition between cycles.

## H. MQ model and credibility lessons from internal training

The internal training deck contributed several strategic ideas without copying licensed research:

- The MQ is a vendor and market evaluation, not a direct product review.
- The companion Critical Capabilities research is important for product and use-case evaluation.
- Inclusion and placement in flagship evaluations are not purchased.
- AR can improve information quality, story clarity, evidence, and analyst credibility.
- The durable result comes from company performance.
- Overstatement can trigger broader scrutiny and reduce credit for legitimate strengths.
- The objective is an accurate reflection of reality, not an unsustainable result above reality.

These principles matter because they shape executive expectations before the cycle begins and when fact review becomes emotional.
